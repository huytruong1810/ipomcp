\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An agent that shares its environment with other agents must predict what they will do, and when the other agents are themselves rational, their actions depend on what they believe, including what they believe about the original agent. Interactive POMDPs (I-POMDPs) \cite{gmytrasiewicz2005framework} formalize this situation from the point of view of an individual agent. They replace the ``flat'' beliefs over physical states used in POMDPs \cite{kaelbling1998planning} with beliefs over interactive states, which combine the physical state with models of the other agents. Intentional models ascribe to the other agent beliefs, preferences and rationality, so the beliefs nest. Finitely nested I-POMDPs bound the nesting at a strategy level $l$ and are the basis for computable approximations.

The price of this expressiveness is computational. Each update of a level-$l$ belief must anticipate every action and observation of the other agent and update the other agent's level-$(l-1)$ belief accordingly, so the support of the interactive belief grows geometrically with time and with nesting. Each anticipated action of the other agent is itself the solution of the other agent's decision problem one level down. Exact solutions are therefore limited to small problems and short horizons \cite{rathnasabapathy2006exact}, and practice relies on sampling: interactive particle filters with reachability-tree sampling \cite{doshi2009monte}, adaptations of POMCP \cite{silver2010monte} to I-POMDPs \cite{hula2015monte}, and nested Monte Carlo tree search \cite{schwartz2022online}. Sampled nested beliefs are brittle: they move in steps of $1/N$, so a modeled agent close to a decision threshold can be predicted to take the wrong action, and under deterministic observations every particle can contradict what the agent perceives, leaving the update undefined.

In this paper we take a different route: the planner computes nested beliefs and the quantities its search integrates exactly wherever this is affordable, and approximates only where it is not. Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{I-POMCP}, an online planner for finitely nested I-POMDPs (Section~\ref{sec:method}). It searches over the agent's own observation histories, carries the agent's interactive belief down the search tree, and integrates immediate rewards and last-step values exactly. Nested beliefs are held exactly, or compressed from exact updates when their support exceeds a bound $N$. After search, the tree is rescored with the exact observation law, which makes the root values exact whenever the tree is complete and yields, at every decision, certified intervals on the exact action values of the searched model.
    \item \textbf{Near-tie escalation} (Section~\ref{sec:escalation}): the planner's own gap between its two best action values identifies the decisions that belief compression can change, and only those are re-decided with a larger belief bound.
    \item \textbf{An evaluation across three domains and nesting levels 0--4} (Section~\ref{sec:results}): the multi-agent tiger game in its published and a coordination variant, UAV reconnaissance, and a two-agent Wumpus world; a comparison with the interactive particle filter with reachability-tree sampling and with nested Monte Carlo tree search; and an analysis, by proposition and in paired episodes, of when an additional level of reasoning pays (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background: Finitely Nested I-POMDPs}
\label{sec:background}

We follow the notation of \citet{gmytrasiewicz2005framework}. For simplicity of presentation we consider an agent $i$ interacting with one other agent, $j$.

\begin{definition}[I-POMDP]
An interactive POMDP of agent $i$ is $\text{I-POMDP}_i = \langle \IS_i, A, T_i, \Omega_i, O_i, R_i \rangle$, where $\IS_i = S \times M_j$ is the set of interactive states, $S$ is the set of physical states and $M_j$ the set of models of agent $j$; $A = A_i \times A_j$ is the set of joint actions; $T_i : S \times A \times S \to [0,1]$ is the transition function; $\Omega_i$ is the set of $i$'s observations and $O_i : S \times A \times \Omega_i \to [0,1]$ its observation function; and $R_i : \IS_i \times A \to \mathbb{R}$ is $i$'s reward function.
\end{definition}

The forms of $T_i$ and $O_i$ reflect the assumptions that agents' actions do not change the other agents' models directly, and that agents cannot observe each other's models. An intentional model of $j$ is a type $\theta_j = \langle b_j, \hth_j \rangle$, consisting of $j$'s belief $b_j$ and its frame $\hth_j = \langle A_j, \Omega_j, T_j, O_j, R_j, OC_j \rangle$. Subintentional models, $SM_j$, are simpler; the no-information model, which assigns equal probability to each of $j$'s actions, is one of them.

\paragraph{Finite nesting.} Finitely nested I-POMDPs are constructed bottom-up:
\begin{align*}
\IS_{i,0} &= S, & M_{j,0} &= \Theta_{j,0} \cup SM_j,\\
\IS_{i,l} &= S \times M_{j,l-1}, & M_{j,l} &= \Theta_{j,l} \cup M_{j,l-1},
\end{align*}
where $\Theta_{j,l} = \{\langle b_{j,l}, \hth_j \rangle : b_{j,l} \in \Delta(\IS_{j,l})\}$. The parameter $l$ is the strategy level. Since $M_{j,l-1}$ contains the models of all lower levels, a level-$l$ agent may be uncertain about the other agent's level as well as about its beliefs. Throughout this paper, level-0 agents follow the no-information model, and the frames of both agents are common knowledge, so the uncertainty of $i$ concerns $j$'s level and $j$'s (nested) beliefs.

\paragraph{Belief update.} After action $a_i^{t-1}$ and observation $o_i^t$, $i$'s new belief, $b_i^t = \SE_{\theta_i}(b_i^{t-1}, a_i^{t-1}, o_i^t)$, is \cite{gmytrasiewicz2005framework}:
\begin{multline}
b_i^t(is^t) = \beta \!\!\sum_{is^{t-1}:\, \hth_j^{t-1} = \hth_j^t}\!\! b_i^{t-1}(is^{t-1}) \sum_{a_j^{t-1}} \Pr(a_j^{t-1} \mid \theta_j^{t-1})\\
\times O_i(s^t, a^{t-1}, o_i^t)\, T_i(s^{t-1}, a^{t-1}, s^t) \sum_{o_j^t} O_j(s^t, a^{t-1}, o_j^t)\\
\times \tau_{\theta_j^t}(b_j^{t-1}, a_j^{t-1}, o_j^t, b_j^t),
\label{eq:update}
\end{multline}
where $\beta$ is a normalizing constant, $\tau_{\theta_j^t}(b_j^{t-1}, a_j^{t-1}, o_j^t, b_j^t)$ is 1 if $b_j^t = \SE_{\theta_j}(b_j^{t-1}, a_j^{t-1}, o_j^t)$ and 0 otherwise, and $\Pr(a_j \mid \theta_j) = 1/|\OPT(\theta_j)|$ if $a_j \in \OPT(\theta_j)$ and 0 otherwise. For a level-0 model, $\Pr(a_j \mid \theta_j) = 1/|A_j|$ and the model does not change. The recursion in $\OPT(\theta_j)$ and in $\SE_{\theta_j}$ terminates at level 0.

\paragraph{Values and solutions.} With $H$ steps to go and discount factor $\gamma$, the value of $\theta_i = \langle b_i, \hth_i \rangle$ is $U^H(\theta_i) = \max_{a_i \in A_i} Q^H(\theta_i, a_i)$, where
\begin{multline}
Q^H(\theta_i, a_i) = \sum_{is} \ER_i(is, a_i)\, b_i(is)\\
+ \gamma \sum_{o_i \in \Omega_i} \Pr(o_i \mid a_i, b_i)\, U^{H-1}\big(\langle \SE_{\theta_i}(b_i, a_i, o_i), \hth_i \rangle\big),
\label{eq:value}
\end{multline}
$\ER_i(is, a_i) = \sum_{a_j} R_i(is, a_i, a_j) \Pr(a_j \mid m_j)$, and $U^0 \equiv 0$. The optimal actions $\OPT(\theta_i)$ are the maximizers of Eq.~\ref{eq:value}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Exact and offline solutions.} Exact solutions of I-POMDPs group the other agent's models into behaviorally equivalent classes, which makes the interactive state space finite \cite{rathnasabapathy2006exact}. Approximate offline methods include interactive point-based value iteration \cite{doshi2008generalized}, bounded policy iteration generalized to I-POMDPs \cite{sonu2015scalable}, and interactive dynamic influence diagrams solved with model equivalences \cite{zeng2012exploiting}. Recursive modeling \cite{gmytrasiewicz2000rational} and the cognitive hierarchy model of games \cite{camerer2004cognitive} motivate uncertainty over the other agent's level. We use the exact finite-horizon I-POMDP, computed by full enumeration, as a reference rather than as a planner.

\paragraph{Sampling-based planning.} The interactive particle filter \cite{doshi2009monte} represents the belief at each nesting level by $N$ sampled particles, weighted by the agent's observation likelihood, and reachability-tree sampling (RTS) looks ahead by expanding $K$ sampled observations per node; together they are the standard approximation for finitely nested I-POMDPs and our first baseline. I-POMDP Lite \cite{hoang2013interactive} assumes that the other agent observes the physical state, so it cannot represent the other agent's beliefs about the original agent. \citet{hula2015monte} adapted POMCP to I-POMDPs to explain human behavior in a trust game. I-NTMCP \cite{schwartz2022online} builds one Monte Carlo search tree per nesting level over particles of states and histories, and predicts the other agent's action by a softmax over visit counts in the tree one level down; it requires only a generative model of the domain, and it is our second baseline. Type-based planners such as POTMMCP \cite{schwartz2023combining} scale online planning to large partially observable games against a finite set of fixed policies, rather than against agents that model the planner. IPOMDP-Net \cite{han2019ipomdpnet} learns an I-POMDP model and plans with a QMDP approximation inside a neural network.

\paragraph{What is new.} To our knowledge, no prior I-POMDP planner holds nested beliefs as exact updates with bounded compression, provides certified bounds on its action values, or maintains uncertainty over the other agent's level jointly with its nested beliefs. \todo{update after a final literature check.}

\section{Interactive POMCP}
\label{sec:method}

I-POMCP (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}) is an online planner: at every step it plans from agent $i$'s current interactive belief with a fixed lookahead $H$, executes the chosen action, and updates the belief with the observation it receives. Its components are a compressed interactive belief filter (Section~\ref{sec:filter}), a Monte Carlo tree search over $i$'s private histories (Section~\ref{sec:search}), exact rescoring of the finished tree with certified intervals (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}), and recursive solutions of the other agent's models (Section~\ref{sec:modeled}).

\subsection{Compressed Interactive Belief Filter}
\label{sec:filter}

The update in Eq.~\ref{eq:update} can be computed exactly when the belief has finite support: it enumerates the support of $b_i^{t-1}$, the actions of $j$ with positive probability under each model, the next physical states and $j$'s observations, and merges interactive states that coincide. Merging requires a canonical form of the interactive state, which we obtain by representing every belief, at every level, as a sorted list of atoms with their probabilities. The difficulty is size: the support may multiply by up to $|A_j|\,|\Omega_j|$ at every step and at every level of nesting.

\begin{definition}[Compressed update]
Given a bound $N$, the compressed update $\SEN_{\theta_i}(b_i, a_i, o_i)$ computes the exact update of Eq.~\ref{eq:update}, in which every nested model $\theta_j$ is advanced by its own compressed update $\SEN_{\theta_j}$, and then, only if the posterior has more than $N$ distinct atoms, replaces it by $N$ stratified draws \cite{douc2005comparison} taken in canonical order, merging duplicates.
\end{definition}

The compressed update has three properties that matter for planning. First, a belief whose support stays within $N$ atoms is updated exactly, so small problems are solved without approximation. Second, the evidence $\Pr(o_i \mid a_i, b_i)$ is computed exactly under the (compressed) prior, so an observation that is possible under the belief is never assigned zero probability, even when sensors are deterministic. Third, the compression error per atom is below $1/N$, and it occurs only where the support exceeds the bound. By contrast, the interactive particle filter \cite{doshi2009monte} represents every belief, however small, by $N$ equally weighted samples and samples every term of every update. Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter} gives the update together with the recursive solution of the other agent's models.

\subsection{Search over Private Histories}
\label{sec:search}

I-POMCP estimates $Q^H(\theta_i, a_i)$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:value} by Monte Carlo tree search \cite{kocsis2006bandit,silver2010monte} over $i$'s private histories $h$, i.e., sequences of $i$'s own actions and observations since the current step (Algorithm~\ref{alg:search}). Unlike POMCP, every node carries $i$'s interactive belief $b_h$, obtained from its parent's belief with the same compressed update used for real observations. Each simulation descends the tree; at history $h$ with $r$ steps to go it selects an action by the upper confidence bound
\begin{equation}
a_i = \arg\max_{a} \Big[ Q(h, a) + c\, W_r \sqrt{\log N(h) / N(h, a)} \Big],
\label{eq:ucb}
\end{equation}
where $W_r = (R_{\max} - R_{\min}) \sum_{t < r} \gamma^t$ is the range of an $r$-step discounted return, computed from the declared bounds of $R_i$, and $c$ is a dimensionless constant. It then draws an interactive state $(s, \theta_j)$ from $b_h$, obtains $j$'s action from the solution of $\theta_j$ (Section~\ref{sec:modeled}), and samples the next physical state and $i$'s observation. Drawing the interactive state from $b_h$ at every step, rather than carrying one sampled state down the tree, makes the observation distribution in the tree equal to $\Pr(o_i \mid a_i, b_h)$, the distribution that the backup integrates.

Two quantities are integrated exactly rather than sampled. The immediate reward at every history is $\ER_i(b_h, a_i) = \sum_{is} \ER_i(is, a_i)\, b_h(is)$, and the last decision ($r = 1$) is valued at $\max_{a_i} \ER_i(b_h, a_i)$, the exact boundary condition of Eq.~\ref{eq:value}. Neither uses the sampled state or $j$'s sampled action, so they add no information that $i$ does not have, and they remove the variance of sampled rewards from the backups. A new child is valued by one rollout with a domain rollout policy that depends only on $i$'s observations. Backups are empirical Bellman backups weighted by the observation counts $C(h, a, o)$:
\begin{equation}
Q(h, a) = \ER_i(b_h, a) + \gamma \sum_{o} \frac{C(h, a, o)}{N(h, a)}\, V(h a o),
\label{eq:backup}
\end{equation}
where $V(h) = \max_a Q(h, a)$ over the actions evaluated at $h$, and a simulated step that ends the episode contributes zero continuation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{I-POMCP decision of agent $i$}
\label{alg:search}
\textbf{Input}: belief $b_i$, frame $\hth_i$, lookahead $H$, simulations $n$\\
\textbf{Output}: action, scored values $Q^\star$, intervals $[Q^-, Q^+]$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE create root history $\emptyset$ with $b_\emptyset \leftarrow b_i$
\FOR{$k = 1, \ldots, n$}
    \STATE \textsc{Simulate}$(\emptyset, H)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $(Q^\star, Q^-, Q^+) \leftarrow$ \textsc{Score}$(\emptyset, H)$ \hfill (Section~\ref{sec:scoring})
\STATE \textbf{return} $a \in \arg\max_{a} Q^\star(\emptyset, a)$, uniformly over ties
\STATE
\STATE \textbf{procedure} \textsc{Simulate}$(h, r)$
\IF{$r = 1$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} $\max_{a} \ER_i(b_h, a)$
\ENDIF
\STATE choose $a_i$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:ucb}
\STATE draw $(s, \theta_j) \sim b_h$; $a_j \sim \Pr(\cdot \mid \theta_j)$ \hfill (Alg.~\ref{alg:filter})
\STATE draw $s' \sim T_i(s, \langle a_i, a_j \rangle, \cdot)$, $o_i \sim O_i(s', \langle a_i, a_j \rangle, \cdot)$
\IF{$h a_i o_i$ is not in the tree}
    \STATE add $h a_i o_i$ with $b_{h a_i o_i} \leftarrow \SEN_{\theta_i}(b_h, a_i, o_i)$
    \STATE $v \leftarrow$ \textsc{Rollout}$(h a_i o_i, r - 1)$
\ELSE
    \STATE $v \leftarrow$ \textsc{Simulate}$(h a_i o_i, r - 1)$
\ENDIF
\STATE increment $N(h)$, $N(h, a_i)$, $C(h, a_i, o_i)$
\STATE update $Q(h, a_i)$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:backup}
\STATE \textbf{return} $Q(h, a_i)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Exact Chance Scoring and Certified Intervals}
\label{sec:scoring}

After the simulations, the finished tree is rescored bottom-up with the exact observation law in place of the sampled frequencies:
\begin{equation}
Q^\star(h, a) = \ER_i(b_h, a) + \gamma \sum_{o} \Pr(o \mid a, b_h)\, V^\star(h a o),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the sum ranges over observations of positive probability, $V^\star(h) = \max_a Q^\star(h, a)$ over the evaluated actions, and $V^\star = \max_a \ER_i(b_h, a)$ at the last decision. With two steps to go, every child of $(h, a)$ is a last decision whose exact value needs only its belief, so such an action is scored over all of its positive observation branches, whether or not search sampled them. With three or more steps to go, an action with a positive-probability observation that search never sampled keeps its search value.

\begin{proposition}[Exact scoring]
\label{prop:exact}
If, at every history of the tree with at least two steps to go, every action of $i$ has been evaluated and every observation of positive probability has a child, then $Q^\star(\emptyset, a) = Q^H(\langle b_i, \hth_i \rangle, a)$ for every $a \in A_i$, where $Q^H$ is computed with the other agent's models solved as in Section~\ref{sec:modeled} and with the compressed update.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By induction on the steps to go. At $r = 1$, $V^\star(h) = \max_a \ER_i(b_h, a) = U^1$. If $V^\star(h a o) = U^{r-1}$ for every positive branch, Eq.~\ref{eq:score} equals Eq.~\ref{eq:value} at $b_h$, since $\Pr(o \mid a, b_h)$ and $\ER_i(b_h, a)$ are exact under $b_h$, and the maximum ranges over all of $A_i$.
\end{proof}

The planner records whether the condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} held, propagating it from the leaves. When it does not, the same pass returns certified intervals. Let $\ell_0 = u_0 = 0$ and, for $r \geq 1$, $\ell_r = R_{\min} + \gamma \min(0, \ell_{r-1})$ and $u_r = R_{\max} + \gamma \max(0, u_{r-1})$; these bound any $r$-step return, including returns of episodes that end early. In the computation of $Q^-$ and $Q^+$, exact terms enter exactly, a rollout frontier with $r$ steps to go enters as $[\ell_r, u_r]$, an unsampled observation branch of probability $p$ enters as $p\,[\ell_{r-1}, u_{r-1}]$, and an action never tried enters as $\ER_i(b_h, a) + \gamma\,[\ell_{r-1}, u_{r-1}]$.

\begin{proposition}[Certified intervals]
\label{prop:bounds}
For every $a \in A_i$, $Q^-(\emptyset, a) \leq Q^H(\langle b_i, \hth_i \rangle, a) \leq Q^+(\emptyset, a)$, where $Q^H$ is as in Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the same induction: every term of Eq.~\ref{eq:value} is either computed exactly or replaced by an interval that contains it, and the maximum of intervals contains the maximum of their members.
\end{proof}

The width of the interval measures the search error that the tree leaves unresolved. It does not account for error in the other agent's model or for compression of the belief.

\subsection{Solving the Other Agent's Models}
\label{sec:modeled}

The probability $\Pr(a_j \mid \theta_j)$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:update} and in the search requires $\OPT(\theta_j)$, i.e., the solution of $j$'s own I-POMDP at level $l-1$. I-POMCP obtains it by running I-POMCP for $j$ at $b_j$, with a separate simulation budget for modeled agents and with $j$'s models of $i$ solved one level further down, until level 0 is reached (Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter}). The modeled agent acts uniformly over the maximizers of its scored values, so it is predicted by the same rule by which $i$ itself decides.

Every solve is seeded from a canonical digest of the modeled belief and the configuration. A modeled policy is therefore a deterministic function of the model, and solutions can be cached across searches and across real steps; interactive states that recur, which is common after the tiger is reset, are not solved again. Since most of the computation is spent on modeled solves, the modeled budget is adaptive: a modeled solve grows one search tree by doubling from a small pilot size and stops as soon as the gap between its two leading actions exceeds a Bonferroni-corrected $t$ critical value times the spread measured by a few independent pilot trees. At its cap, the solve is identical to a flat search, so the rule is consistent.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Compressed update and modeled solves}
\label{alg:filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{procedure} \textsc{Policy}$(\theta_j)$ \hfill ($\theta_j$ at level $k$)
\IF{$k = 0$}
    \STATE \textbf{return} the uniform distribution over $A_j$
\ENDIF
\IF{the canonical digest of $\theta_j$ is cached}
    \STATE \textbf{return} the cached policy
\ENDIF
\STATE run Algorithm~\ref{alg:search} for $j$ at $b_j$ with the modeled budget, solving $j$'s models of $i$ by \textsc{Policy} at level $k-1$
\STATE \textbf{return} the uniform distribution over $\arg\max_{a_j} Q^\star$, and cache it
\STATE
\STATE \textbf{procedure} $\SEN_{\theta_i}(b_i, a_i, o_i)$
\STATE $b' \leftarrow 0$
\FORALL{$(s, \theta_j) \in \mathrm{supp}(b_i)$, $a_j$ with $\pi = \textsc{Policy}(\theta_j)(a_j) > 0$, $s'$, $o_j$}
    \STATE $w \leftarrow b_i(s, \theta_j)\, \pi\, T_i(s, a, s')\, O_i(s', a, o_i)\, O_j(s', a, o_j)$
    \STATE $\theta_j' \leftarrow \langle \SEN_{\theta_j}(b_j, a_j, o_j), \hth_j \rangle$ \hfill (unchanged at level 0)
    \STATE $b'(s', \theta_j') \leftarrow b'(s', \theta_j') + w$ \hfill (canonical merge)
\ENDFOR
\STATE normalize $b'$
\IF{$|\mathrm{supp}(b')| > N$}
    \STATE $b' \leftarrow$ $N$ stratified draws from $b'$ in canonical order, merged
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{return} $b'$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Finally, we keep positive probability on level 0 in every prior over levels. With deterministic observations, a modeled $j$ whose model of $i$ is concentrated on one level can receive an observation that this model deems impossible, leaving $\SE_{\theta_j}$ undefined; since the no-information model gives every action positive probability, level-0 mass keeps the update defined whenever the observation is possible at all.

\subsection{Near-Tie Escalation}
\label{sec:escalation}

Compression with a small bound $N$ can change a decision only where the decision is close. The planner measures closeness itself: the gap between its two largest scored values, $Q^\star(\emptyset, a_{(1)}) - Q^\star(\emptyset, a_{(2)})$. Each agent at level 2 or higher therefore keeps a \emph{shadow}: the same planner, with the same seeds, at a larger bound $N' > N$, conditioned on the same realized actions and observations and updated at every real step. When the base planner's top-two gap is at most a threshold $g$, the shadow decides instead; otherwise the base planner's decision stands. A modeled agent is solved by the base planner only, so escalation changes the agent's real decisions but not its predictions of others. We use $N = 64$, $N' = 1{,}024$ at level 2 and $N' = 256$ at levels 3 and 4, and $g = 3$ in reward units. \todo{share of decisions escalated and cost relative to the uniform larger bound (results).}

\section{When Does a Deeper Model Pay?}
\label{sec:depth}

A higher strategy level is more expensive, and it is natural to ask when it is worth its cost. Fix the real agent $j$ and the lookahead $H$. We call two models of $j$ \emph{predictively equivalent} at a history of $i$ when they induce the same joint distribution over the next $H$ steps of $j$'s actions and $i$'s observations.

\begin{proposition}[Correct prediction suffices]
\label{prop:prediction}
If $i$'s model of $j$ is predictively equivalent to the real $j$ at every history of the next $H$ steps, then $i$'s exact solution maximizes $i$'s true expected $H$-step reward at the current decision, and no other model of $j$, of any level or prior, does better at that decision.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given the predictive distribution, $i$'s planning problem is a POMDP over the physical state and $j$'s internal state, whose transition and observation functions are fixed by that distribution. Predictively equivalent models induce the same POMDP and hence the same $Q^H$, which the exact solution maximizes.
\end{proof}

In particular, if $j$'s actions do not depend on any belief about $i$, as for the no-information model, a level-1 agent that models $j$ correctly is already optimal, and a deeper agent that places probability on intentional models of $j$ can only lose. An additional level can pay only when the real agent's actions depend on its beliefs about $i$, and only when the payoffs make $i$'s best response sensitive to those actions. The proposition concerns a single decision with lookahead $H$; receding-horizon episodes carry no such guarantee, which is why we test it empirically (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Domains}

\paragraph{Multi-agent tiger.} Two agents face two doors, one hiding a tiger and the other a prize \cite{gmytrasiewicz2005framework}. Each agent can listen or open a door; listening costs 1, opening the tiger's door costs 100 and opening the other door pays 10, and if either agent opens a door the tiger is placed at random. A listening agent hears the tiger's growl from the correct side with probability 0.85, together with a creak that reports the other agent's action with probability 0.9. In this published game each agent's payoff depends only on its own door, so modeling the other agent can change $i$'s best response only through the state. We therefore also use a \emph{coordination} variant with the same states, transitions and observations, in which an agent that opens the safe door in the same step as the other agent receives 40 instead of 10. The joint payoff was chosen so that the exact value of one more level of reasoning is positive at reachable beliefs; the published game serves as a control.

\paragraph{UAV reconnaissance.} An unmanned aerial vehicle $i$ seeks a ground target $j$ on a $3 \times 3$ grid of sectors \cite{doshi2009monte}. Both agents move to an adjacent sector or stay; the UAV knows its own sector and, when it stays, observes the target's row with probability 0.8; the target observes the UAV's row with probability 0.6. The UAV receives 50 for spotting the target, which ends the episode, and pays 5 for every other step; the target loses 50 when spotted. Parameters the original description leaves open follow \citet{zeng2012exploiting}.

\paragraph{Two-agent Wumpus world.} A human $i$ searches a $3 \times 3$ grid with one pit for gold, while a wumpus $j$ hunts the human. The human can move forward, turn, grab and shoot one arrow; the wumpus can move forward and turn. Both agents perceive deterministic sets of tokens (the human: bump, stench, breeze, glitter and scream; the wumpus: bump, breeze, glitter and the human's footsteps within distance 2). The human receives 1000 for the gold and $-1000$ for dying, and the wumpus 1000 for catching the human and $-1000$ for being shot; every other step costs 1. Deterministic percepts make this domain a stress test for sampled beliefs.

\subsection{Exact References}

\paragraph{Exact I-POMDP.} For a single decision, we solve the finite-horizon I-POMDP of Eq.~\ref{eq:value} by full enumeration, with every lower level solved exactly in turn and with exact beliefs. A modeled agent with several optimal actions plays them uniformly, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:update}. As a measure of a decision with policy $\pi$ at belief $b_i$ we use the first-action loss $U^H(\theta_i) - \sum_{a} \pi(a)\, Q^H(\theta_i, a)$, the value lost by the decision if every later decision is optimal, computed in the exact I-POMDP. A decision is \emph{optimal} when its loss is at most $10^{-9}$.

\paragraph{The exact agent along realized histories.} In whole episodes, each agent's realized actions and observations are replayed through the \emph{exact} (Bayes-optimal) I-POMDP agent of the same level, which acts on $\OPT(\theta_i)$ at every step with every level of the hierarchy solved exactly, with the same frames, level priors and lookahead. At every step we compare the planner's action with $\OPT$ of the exact agent, the total variation distance between the two agents' posteriors over the other agent's level, and the total variation distance between their predictions of the other agent's action. Exact beliefs grow quickly, so this replay covers the first steps of each episode (the \emph{exact window}): \todo{window lengths per level}.

\paragraph{Exact expected reward of a pair.} Inside the exact window, the expected cumulative reward of a pair of exact agents is computed with zero variance by enumerating the joint process of the physical state and both agents' beliefs forward in time, merging equal nodes.

\subsection{Baselines}

\paragraph{IPF + RTS.} The interactive particle filter with reachability-tree sampling \cite{doshi2009monte}, with $N = 64$ particles at every level and $K \in \{2, 4, 8\}$ sampled observations per node, both as published and with likelihood smoothing (each particle weighted by $(1-\epsilon) O_i + \epsilon / |\Omega_i|$, $\epsilon = 0.01$), the standard repair for particles of zero likelihood.

\paragraph{I-NTMCP.} The nested Monte Carlo tree search of \citet{schwartz2022online}, run with the authors' implementation through an adapter to our domains, both as published and with tuned parameters. \todo{state the tuning protocol.}

All planners are implemented in Python and run single-threaded, one process per decision, and we report process CPU time per decision with nested solves included.

\subsection{Protocols and Measures}

\paragraph{Decisions.} On each domain, level and horizon we build a grid of problems: for the tiger games, $i$'s belief about the tiger crossed with $j$'s belief and, at level 3 and above, with the beliefs of the models below $j$; for UAV, named distributions over the other agent's sector at each level; for the Wumpus world, beliefs reachable from the common prior. Each planner decides every problem, and each decision is scored against the exact I-POMDP. We report the share of optimal decisions with exact Clopper--Pearson 95\% intervals \cite{clopper1934use}, and compare planners on the same problems with exact McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947note}, Holm-corrected \cite{holm1979simple} within each family of comparisons. Budgets are chosen on a selection grid and then tested, unchanged, on beliefs and seeds that the selection did not use.

\paragraph{Episodes over the level grid.} In both tiger games, agent $i$ at levels 0--4 plays against $j$ at levels 0--4 in episodes of 10 steps, with lookahead $H = 2$, $\gamma = 0.95$, $N = 64$, and a uniform prior over the other agent's lower levels at every level of nesting. Both games are symmetric in the agents, so the cells with $i$'s level at least $j$'s determine the whole grid. \todo{trials per cell.}

\paragraph{Rao-Blackwellized rewards.} The agents' policies depend on the physical state only through their observations. Conditioned on both agents' actions and observations over an episode, the physical state therefore follows a hidden Markov model, and each sampled reward can be replaced by its exact conditional expectation, computed by forward--backward recursion over the physical states. By the tower property the estimand is unchanged, and the variance can only decrease \cite{casella1996rao}. The primary endpoint is the paired gain from one more level of $i$ against the same $j$, paired by trial seed.

\paragraph{Anytime episodes.} Beyond the reach of exact solutions, we compare planners in 20-step UAV episodes by reward against CPU time per decision, and score the early decisions of each episode exactly. \todo{horizons and budgets.}

\subsection{Whom Does Each Agent Believe It Faces?}
\label{sec:identification}

An agent of level $l$ maintains a posterior over the other agent's level, the marginal of its interactive belief over $M_{j,l-1}$. Figure~\ref{fig:identification} shows this posterior over the level grid. One observation looks puzzling at first: a level-2 agent comes to believe that the other agent is of level 1 whether the other agent is in fact of level 1, 2, 3 or 4 (panels a and c). After 10 steps it assigns 0.91--0.95 to level 1 in both games, against 0.08 when the other agent is random. Three findings explain this behavior.

\paragraph{The observer converges to its closest hypothesis.} A level-2 agent can represent only two hypotheses about the other agent: the no-information model (level 0) and level 1. When the true model is not among the hypotheses, the posterior concentrates on the hypothesis whose predictions are closest to the truth in Kullback--Leibler divergence \cite{berk1966limiting}, not on the least informative one. Every planning agent listens until the growls agree and then opens a door, which level 1 predicts and uniform play does not. Against an agent that does play at random, the same observer's belief in level 1 falls to 0.08 [0.07, 0.09] ($n = 2{,}500$, published game; 0.08 with $n = 15{,}000$ in the coordination game).

\paragraph{In the published game, deeper agents do not act differently.} Table~\ref{tab:fixedpoint} gives, from exact solutions, the share of belief chains in the published game where the optimal actions of level $k+1$ differ from those of level $k$. From level 2 upward the hierarchy is at a fixed point: an agent of level $k+2$ best-responds to an agent of level $k+1$ that acts exactly as one of level $k$, so by induction an agent of any level $l \geq 2$ acts as one of level 2 at this lookahead. The episodes agree. Against a random opponent, the planner at levels 2 and 3 takes the same 10-step action sequence in 90\% of 700 paired trials, and where the two diverge, the level-3 agent's own top-two value gap is at most 1 in 88\% of cases, i.e., the divergences are near-ties broken differently. By contrast, in the coordination game levels 1 and 2 diverge in 24\% of trials against a random opponent and 62\% against a level-1 opponent, and the level-2 agent's median top-two gap at the first divergence is 7.4 and 8.3, respectively; this is where an additional level pays (Section~\ref{sec:depth}).

\paragraph{Levels are distinguished when they are represented and act differently.} A level-3 observer, whose hypotheses are levels 0--2 (panels b and d), assigns 0.90 to level 0 when the other agent is random, and its posterior mode is the true level when the other agent is of level 1 (0.49 against 0.45 for level 2 in the published game; 0.52 against 0.42 in the coordination game) or of level 2 (0.50 against 0.45; 0.53 against 0.43). An agent of level 3 or 4, which the observer cannot represent, is assigned to level 2, its closest representable model (0.49--0.59), and never to level 0 (at most 0.06). The separation between levels 1 and 2 is modest because the two act identically in most histories. For the same reason, a level-4 observer in the published game keeps 0.27--0.35 on each of levels 1--3 whatever the other agent's level: levels that act identically cannot be told apart by any observer, so this spread reflects non-identifiability, not an inference error.

\paragraph{The posteriors are Bayes-exact.} Replayed along the same histories through the exact agent, the planner's posterior over the other agent's level differs from the exact posterior by a mean total variation of 0.004--0.012 per observer and cell (maximum 0.27), and by less than 0.01 in 67--83\% of decisions, over 300--1{,}600 decisions per cell.
